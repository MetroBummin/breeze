Starship Finally Delivered. Now Comes the Hard Part.

By Owen Delaney

For four years, the largest rocket ever built had one persistent problem: it had never actually carried anything. Starship flew, and sometimes it exploded, and each flight taught SpaceX something new about how to keep a stainless-steel skyscraper from tearing itself apart. But it had never done the ordinary thing that rockets exist to do, which is to put a useful object into space and leave it there.

On the evening of July 24, that finally changed. Starship lifted off from Starbase, on the southern tip of Texas, at 5:51 local time, and roughly forty minutes later it opened a slot in its side and released twenty Starlink satellites. They were the first operational payloads the vehicle had ever deployed. Six of them carried cameras pointed back at the ship, so that engineers could watch their own heat shield from the outside.

The flight was not flawless, and the imperfection was instructive.

A tale of two stages

Starship is really two vehicles stacked on top of each other. The lower one, called Super Heavy, does the brutal early work of pushing the whole stack off the ground, then falls away and comes home. The upper one, confusingly also called Starship, carries the cargo.

The upper stage performed beautifully. All six of its engines ran cleanly during the climb. Once in space, it relit a single engine and shut it down again — a small maneuver that sounds trivial and is not, because relighting a rocket engine in vacuum, after it has been soaked in cold, is precisely the capability the vehicle will need to leave orbit and land on the Moon. Sixty-five minutes after launch, the ship came down under power into the Indian Ocean off the coast of Western Australia, tipped over onto the water, and simply stayed there. It did not explode. No Starship had ever managed that before, and it mattered: a floating, intact vehicle can be inspected by drones, and an inspection tells you things that telemetry cannot.

The booster had a worse evening. Coming back toward the Gulf of Mexico, it was supposed to relight thirteen engines to slow itself down. Only ten lit. By the moment it reached the water, only about five appeared to be running, and the stage hit the surface far harder than intended and was destroyed. It landed on target, which is not nothing — the guidance worked. The propulsion did not.

That failure had a rehearsal. Eight days earlier, on the first launch attempt, the flight computer had called off the countdown at the last possible instant, with the clock at zero, after six engines reported trouble with the pumps that feed them liquid oxygen. SpaceX scrubbed, investigated, and flew again. Whether the two problems share a root cause is the question engineers in Texas are now working through.

Catching a skyscraper

The next flight is where the ambition becomes almost absurd. Elon Musk has said that Flight 14, targeted for late August, will attempt something the program has never tried: catching the upper stage in mid-air with the enormous mechanical arms on the launch tower, the pair that SpaceX engineers nicknamed Mechazilla.

They have caught the booster this way before. Catching the ship is a different proposition, because the ship must first go all the way to orbit and then come back through the atmosphere, arriving at the tower after a punishing reentry rather than a short hop. Flight 14 would therefore be the first genuinely orbital Starship mission as well as the first catch attempt. It also depends on regulators agreeing, which is not automatic.

The reason for all of this is economics. A rocket you catch and refly is worth far more than one you fish out of the ocean, and Starship's entire business case rests on flying often and cheaply. The V3 Starlink satellites it is now carrying are said to offer roughly ten times the bandwidth of the current generation, and they are too large and too heavy to launch any other way.

Government auditors remain unconvinced about the schedule. A recent report noted that SpaceX still faces major challenges transferring propellant between vehicles in orbit — an unglamorous problem, and the one that stands between Starship and the Moon — and that the program is more than a year behind where it promised to be.

Still, something changed on July 24. For the first time, the question about Starship stopped being whether it can survive the trip, and started being whether it can be made routine.
