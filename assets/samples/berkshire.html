The Quiet Canadian Who Took Buffett's Chair

By Marta Bevan

Succession at most companies is a matter of org charts. At Berkshire Hathaway it was closer to a question of theology. For sixty years the company had one chief executive, and that man was not merely a manager but the author of a philosophy that several million people had adopted as their own. When Warren Buffett handed the job to Greg Abel on the first day of this year, he was handing over something stranger than a corporation.

Abel is sixty-three, Canadian, and almost aggressively unremarkable in public. He grew up in Edmonton, trained as an accountant, and spent two decades running Berkshire's energy business, a sprawling collection of utilities and pipelines that is profitable, essential, and very difficult to make interesting at a party. He is not a showman. He has given no memorable interviews. This appears to have been among his qualifications.

What changed immediately

The reassuring assumption was that nothing would change. That assumption lasted about a quarter.

In his first months, Abel sold out of sixteen positions entirely and trimmed six others — a rate of turnover that would have been unthinkable under a man who once said his favourite holding period was forever. He has also shown an appetite for technology companies, a category Buffett spent most of his career explaining that he did not understand and therefore would not buy. And he has been shopping abroad, apparently on the view that American shares had simply become too expensive to offer the kind of margin Berkshire has always demanded.

His first large acquisition was domestic, though. In a deal worth about $6.8 billion, Berkshire bought Taylor Morrison, one of the country's bigger homebuilders. It was, in structure, an entirely Buffett-like purchase: an unfashionable business, bought whole, in an industry the company already understood. In timing and scale, it was a statement.

The part that cannot be inherited

Buffett, now ninety-five and still chairman, has been generous in public. Abel, he wrote, has more than met the high expectations he had for him when he first concluded that Abel should be Berkshire's next chief executive. Coming from a man who has spent decades choosing his words in shareholder letters with the care of a poet, that is not faint praise.

Yet the hardest thing Abel inherited is not the portfolio, which is roughly $355 billion and concentrated — nearly two-thirds of it sits in just five companies. It is the reason Berkshire exists in its current form at all. Families sold their businesses to Buffett, often below the price a private equity firm would have paid, because they trusted him not to dismantle what they had built. That trust was personal. It does not transfer automatically with a title, and it cannot be bought back once spent.

Abel's answer so far has been to behave as though he is being watched, which he is. He has kept the decentralised structure that lets Berkshire's dozens of subsidiaries run themselves. He has not moved the headquarters out of Omaha. He has not hired a communications team to explain him.

The real test will arrive the first time the market falls hard. Buffett's genuine talent was never stock-picking alone; it was the temperament to buy when everyone else was selling, and the balance sheet to make that possible. Berkshire is still sitting on an enormous pile of cash, waiting, as it always has, for someone else's panic.

Whether Abel has the same stomach is the only question that matters, and it is one that no amount of preparation can answer in advance. He will find out at the same moment everyone else does.
